agent-wrapper · design system · color

Status is the only
reason to spend color.

The app's own status palette is the honest source. Green, amber and red are spent on states the app actually knows — never on decoration. Everything else is neutral, and the single accent is a deepened terminal cyan, borrowed from the ANSI world the app already lives in.

Rules

Five that decide everything else

  • Status color is earned, never decorative.

    Green, amber and red appear only where the app is reporting a state it actually knows. Not on a heading, not on a chart series, not to make a button feel positive. If you want a color and there is no state behind it, the answer is the accent or a neutral.

  • Running is accent, not green.

    Green is a verdict, and a run in progress has not reached one. This is why the accent had to be cyan rather than a hue chosen for taste — it has to sit beside the status trio without being read as one of them.

  • Never color alone.

    Every status carries a glyph and a word. Color is the third channel, not the first. Here that is load-bearing rather than a nicety — the evidence is two sections down.

  • One accent, no second brand hue.

    Two accents and the eye starts assigning meaning to the difference. Emphasis comes from --c-accent-strong, weight and space.

  • Neutrals carry the UI.

    Nearly every surface, border and glyph is neutral. Color is the exception — which is exactly what makes it legible when it does appear.

States

What the app knows

Every state maps to a token in one place, so a new state is a decision made once rather than a color picked at a call site. Three of seven states get a hue; the rest stay muted.

Evidence

Why “never color alone” is a hard rule

The right-hand column is the same status list under a deuteranopia simulation — the most common color vision deficiency, around 6% of men. It is applied live, to these actual token values.

Running and Done collapse to 1.25:1 — effectively one color. We tested the alternatives: shifting green yellow-ward reaches 1.47:1, still indistinguishable. No hue pair solves this at these lightnesses while keeping a cyan accent. And running and done are adjacent rows in the session list — the two states a user most needs to tell apart at a glance — so the separation cannot live in the color. It lives in the glyph, the label, and the fact that running is the only state that animates. The palette is sound; the redundancy is what makes it safe.

Passthrough

The agent's colors, and where ours stop

Agent output arrives carrying its own SGR escape codes. We don't restyle it — that would misrepresent what the wrapped agent said. We only decide what the sixteen slots resolve to inside --c-sunken. That surface is the boundary: inside it the agent's colors, outside it ours.

  • In light mode the bright half deepens rather than lightens.

    Slot 10 is #114B26, darker than slot 2's #196634. Bright means emphasis, and on a light ground emphasis is more contrast, not less.

  • Slot 0 in dark mode is a fill, not a foreground.

    At 1.49:1 it is unreadable as text, so the renderer lifts foreground ANSI black to slot 8 rather than emitting invisible output. It is the only lift needed — the other fifteen slots clear 4.5:1 on --c-sunken in both themes.

  • Slot 6 is the accent, in both themes.

    That is the join. The app's accent isn't imposed on the terminal — it's a color the terminal already had.

Enforcement

The numbers are checked, not claimed

design/contrast.js parses the token values straight out of color.css and asserts every pairing on this page, so the documentation cannot drift from what ships. It also asserts the invariants the rules depend on — that slot 6 equals the accent, that running resolves to the accent and not to ok, and that the bright half moves the right direction in each theme. It exits non-zero on a regression.
